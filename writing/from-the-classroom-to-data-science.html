From the classroom to data science

25 Oct 2026 · 5 min read

On paper, going from teaching mathematics to data science looks like a big change. From the inside, it has felt less like a jump and more like a slow turn. I have been standing in front of classes since 2012, first at Mdumiseni High School and since 2017 at Majuba TVET College. Somewhere along the way the questions I cared about in the classroom started to look a lot like data questions.

It started with a mark sheet

Every term ends with a spreadsheet of marks. For a long time I treated it as the end of the story: the results are in, the term is over. Then I started looking at the numbers during the term instead of after it. Which students were slipping? Which topic did the whole class get wrong, which usually says more about my teaching than about them?

None of that was data science. But it was the same instinct: look at the evidence early, while there is still time to do something about it.

Learning in layers

I did not make the move in one go. I completed a BSc in Mathematics and Computer Science through UNISA alongside my teaching, then the ALX Data Analyst and Data Science programmes, and now a Postgraduate Diploma in Data Science at STADIO.

Learning that way is slow, and some evenings it is tiring. The upside is that each new idea had somewhere to land. When I met classification models, I already knew what it felt like to decide which learners needed extra help with limited time. When I met confidence intervals, I had spent years telling students that one test score is not the whole picture.

What carried over

Explaining things to people who did not ask to learn them. Most of my students do not love mathematics. Getting an idea across to someone who would rather be elsewhere is good practice for presenting a model to a manager who has five minutes and one question: what should we do?

Fairness in measurement. I helped build assessment rubrics so that a mark means the same thing whoever does the marking. A good metric needs the same property. If it shifts depending on who runs the analysis, it is not much use.

Working within capacity. A lecturer cannot give every student an hour of extra help each week. You choose, and you try to choose well. That is the same question as a fraud team that can review 300 transactions a day, or a retention team that can only call one customer in ten.

What I had to learn from scratch

Plenty. The biggest surprise was how easily data can mislead you without looking wrong. A model can score beautifully because it has quietly seen the future, or because the test data is too similar to the training data. Learning to split data by time, check for leakage and compare against a simple baseline took real practice. I wrote about the baseline habit in Beat the baseline first.

The other lesson was that a notebook is not a result. A chart I find interesting does not help anyone until it is turned into a decision someone can make on Monday morning. That is why each of my projects ends with a recommendation rather than a score.

Your previous career is not a gap on your CV. It is the context you bring to the data.

If you are making a similar move

A few things I would tell myself a few years ago:

  • Start with questions you already have at work. They are easier to care about, and you will understand the data better than most.
  • Build fewer projects and finish them properly, with a clear question, an honest test and a recommendation at the end.
  • Write about what you learn. Explaining it to someone else is the quickest way to find out what you do not understand yet.
  • Do not hide where you came from. Teaching, nursing, accounting, retail: each of them teaches you something about people and decisions that a course cannot.

Still both

I do not think I will ever stop being a teacher, and I am certainly still a student. Some days I am the one explaining, and some days I am the one stuck on an error message I do not understand yet. I think that is a good place to be. If you are on a similar path, or you are hiring someone who is, I would be glad to hear from you.
