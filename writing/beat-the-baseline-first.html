Beat the baseline first

24 Oct 2026 · 4 min read

A model on its own tells you very little. Saying a forecast is 3.1% off sounds good, but good compared to what? Every project I build starts by asking what someone would do without the model, and then checking whether the model actually does better.

What a baseline is

A baseline is the simplest reasonable answer to the question. For a demand forecast it is "next week will look like this week". For a call list it is "phone people at random", or "phone the ones most likely to leave". It costs almost nothing to build, and it is often what the team is doing already.

If my model cannot beat that, it does not matter how clever it is.

Forecasting electricity demand

In my Eskom demand forecasting project I compared four approaches over 52 weeks of walk-forward testing:

Approach (week ahead)Error
Same as last week (seasonal naive)3.6%
Average of the last four weeks3.7%
Calendar regression5.2%
Gradient boosting3.1%

Two things stand out. The final model is about 15% better than "same as last week", which is a real but modest gain. And the calendar regression, which looks more sophisticated than the naive baseline, was clearly worse. Without the baseline I might have shipped it.

Ranking a churn call list

Baselines are not only about accuracy. In the churn project the retention team can only call about 10% of customers. Calling at random protects about 10% of the revenue at risk. Ranking by churn probability gets to about 58%. Ranking by expected loss, probability times what the customer is worth, protects about 82%.

The same model sits behind both rankings. The baseline comparison showed that how you use the scores mattered as much as the scores themselves.

Sometimes the answer is "don't"

In the A/B test, the new landing page was compared with the old one, which is the baseline. Conversion moved by −0.16 points, and the whole 95% confidence interval sat below the smallest lift worth acting on. The right recommendation was to keep the old page. A baseline protects you from shipping change for its own sake.

Before asking how good a model is, ask what it beats.

The habit

I learned this in the classroom before I learned it in Python. When a new teaching method seems to work, the first question is whether the class would have improved anyway. The tools are different now, but the question is the same.
